AI automation for London businesses

I am an AI automation consultant in London. I take the repetitive admin off your team using AI and custom software, and I build the tools to do it. Here is what I do, who I help, and why it is worth a conversation.

What gets automated, in practice

"Business automation" covers a lot of ground, so here is the concrete version. Almost every job I am asked to look at falls into one of six shapes. They turn up in different clothes depending on the industry, but underneath they are the same problem.

  1. Documents arriving that someone has to read and file. Invoices, signed forms, certificates, ID, supplier paperwork. Someone opens each one, works out what it is, pulls a few fields off it and puts it somewhere. This is the clearest case for AI, because the reading step is the bit that used to need a person and now does not.
  2. The same information typed into two places. A job goes into the operations system, then again into the accounts package. A client's details go into the CRM, then into a spreadsheet, then into a letter. Every retype is a chance to introduce an error that someone finds three weeks later.
  3. Reports rebuilt by hand every week or month. The same figures pulled from the same places into the same layout, by the same person, every single month. Month end is where I most often find a full day of work that nobody has ever questioned.
  4. Dates that have to be watched. Renewals, expiry dates, deadlines, review dates, compliance certificates. These usually live in a spreadsheet with conditional formatting, or in the head of whoever has been there longest. Date-driven work is the easiest kind to automate reliably.
  5. Chasing. Chasing clients for documents, suppliers for updates, colleagues for sign-off. It is low-skill, it is endless, and it is almost always done by someone whose time is worth considerably more than chasing.
  6. Onboarding, of a client or a member of staff. A fixed sequence of forms, checks, accounts to create and things to send, run manually from a checklist, slightly differently every time.

If you recognise three or more of those, there is almost certainly a week's worth of your team's time sitting in them.

When automation is not worth it

I would rather say this here than after you have paid me. Some work should be left alone, and the honest reasons are usually these.

  • It does not happen often enough. A task that takes twenty minutes twice a year is not worth building anything for, however annoying it is.
  • The process changes every time it runs. If there is no repeatable shape, there is nothing to automate yet. The fix there is to agree the process first, which costs nothing.
  • The judgement is the job. If the valuable part is a person weighing something up, automating around it is fine, automating it away is not.
  • Something off the shelf already does it. If a product on the market fits how you work, buy the product. I have written a guide on making that call, and it says buy more often than people expect.
  • The process is about to change anyway. If you are mid-way through switching systems, wait. Building against something you are replacing is money burnt.

This is what the free first call is for. If there is nothing worth building, I will tell you that, and it costs you nothing to find out.
